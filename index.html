Overnight, in two lines

Yemen's Saudi-backed forces pulled back from the Bab al-Mandab Strait on Sunday to defend Aden, leaving the Iran-aligned Houthis in control of the shore along the Red Sea's southern gateway. The same day Iran's Revolutionary Guards said they sent six drones at a US radar in Bahrain, their second claimed attack there in three days. Bahrain says it shot them down. With Hormuz already shut, both of the region's oil chokepoints are now held or contested by Iran and its allies.

Asian stocks fell at Monday's open. Taiwan dropped 4.7% and Japan's Nikkei 1.7% after Japan's GDP shrank and China's October data missed forecasts across the board, two days before Nvidia reports. In Washington, Democrats pushed back on the administration's $50 billion air traffic control request by pointing to money that is still unspent. Italy fined Meta $887 million over Facebook's access to Android photos.

Geopolitics · Shipping chokepoints

The Houthis take the Bab al-Mandab shore as Iran hits at Bahrain again: both Gulf oil exits now under pressure

BBC · Al Jazeera · France 24 · NBC · The National · Washington Post · Xinhua · Nov 15
Houthis ~60 km from Aden (France 24)6 drones claimed, Bahrain~330,000 displaced in Yemen (UN)Brent $95.14

What happened in Yemen. The Southern Armed Forces, which fight for Yemen's internationally recognized government with Saudi backing, said on Sunday they were withdrawing "from Dhubab Airport and the Bab al-Mandab area" to reinforce Aden, the interim capital. Houthi-run media later said the group had taken the territory, and Saudi Arabia has not commented (BBC). The pullback ends a weeks-long effort to win back ground the Houthis took in a surprise offensive in September, including Perim Island at the mouth of the strait and the port of Mokha. France 24, citing military sources, says Houthi forces have advanced to within about 60 km of Aden. A four-year informal ceasefire began breaking down in July, when the Houthis declared a "maritime embargo" on Saudi Arabia. Since then they have launched hundreds of drones and missiles at Saudi cities and oil facilities, and Saudi Arabia has answered with hundreds of air strikes. The Houthis say they pose no threat to international shipping in general but will keep targeting Saudi vessels.

What happened in the Gulf. The IRGC said it launched six drones at a US air-defense radar at Bahrain's Riffa Air Base, calling it the "second phase" of retaliation for what it called US aggression. Bahrain sounded sirens twice before dawn and said its air defenses stopped several Iranian "aerial attacks" (Al Jazeera). The US military had not commented on Sunday's claim. On Friday, US Central Command (CENTCOM) said it shot down two drones with no damage, contradicting Iran's claim to have destroyed a radar. Separately, the IRGC navy says tankers keep hitting mines on what it calls "unauthorized" routes through Hormuz. Iran says the strait remains closed, and the US rejects Iran's claim to control it. Talks are stalled: Iran's chief negotiator says Tehran sent seven conditions through Qatar, including lifting the US naval blockade, and is waiting for a reply. Washington has instead tightened sanctions.

Why it matters (analysis)With Hormuz shut, Saudi Arabia has been sending its oil exports out through the Red Sea, so who controls Bab al-Mandab matters well beyond Yemen. The BBC notes the Houthis' September seizure already pushed oil prices up. Markets reacted calmly on Monday: Brent slipped as traders focused on expectations of a supply surplus. That suggests the risk is being judged by attacks on actual ships, not by who holds the shoreline. In Bahrain, the attacks so far appear calibrated and have been intercepted. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera say the main danger is miscalculation, as tit-for-tat strikes could turn into a wider conflict neither side planned. Both governments' accounts of the damage are unverified.

Markets · Macro

Asia opens the week lower: Japan contracts, China misses on every major gauge, and chipmakers slide before Nvidia

Reuters · CNBC · Nikkei Asia · NHK · Japan Cabinet Office · China NBS · Nov 16
Nikkei −1.7%Taiwan −4.7%Kospi −3%Hang Seng +0.8%

The data. Japan's economy shrank 0.02% in July–September, or 0.1% annualized. It was the first contraction in four quarters and missed expectations (Cabinet Office, Nikkei Asia). Private consumption fell 0.3% as inflation squeezed households, and net exports cut 0.6 points from growth as energy imports rose. Business investment grew 1.6%. China's October figures all came in below forecasts (CNBC). Retail sales grew 0.4% year on year against 0.8% expected. Industrial output grew 4.8% against 5.3% expected. Fixed-asset investment fell 8.1% over the first ten months, a decline CNBC calls unprecedented outside the pandemic. Exports remain the bright spot, up 35.2% in October, and China's trade surplus passed $1 trillion faster than ever.

The market. MSCI's Asia-Pacific index excluding Japan fell 1.9%. TSMC, which makes most of Nvidia's AI chips, lost 4% (Reuters). Nvidia's market value has dropped to about $3.9 trillion from about $5.8 trillion in early October, according to LSEG data, as investors left the stock after Jensen Huang's exit. Analysts expect revenue of $109 billion in Tuesday's results. Options imply a move of about 7% either way, and Pepperstone's Chris Weston said investors are now trying to value Nvidia "potentially without the individual who architected its competitive advantage." S&P 500 futures were down 0.2% and Nasdaq futures down 0.4%. The 10-year Treasury yield held at 5.14% after jumping 29 basis points last week.

Why it matters (analysis)Three things are converging this week: weak Asian demand, US inflation that keeps rate hikes priced in (a quarter-point increase in December is essentially certain, per futures), and a test of the AI trade through Nvidia's results on Tuesday and Anthropic's pricing on Wednesday. Pinpoint's Zhiwei Zhang told CNBC that China's export strength depends heavily on AI-equipment demand, so any slowdown in AI spending would hit growth well beyond the US. Bulls point to Nvidia's expected 90% earnings growth. Bears point to the leadership vacuum and to bond yields above 5%.

Cursor · The Hacker News · BleepingComputer · Nov 13–15

Cursor traces its breach to a key exposed by inference vendor Fireworks, which led to a trojanized Claude Code extension

≤5,000 users exposed to bad build~11 hours liveContained since Nov 1

In a post-incident report, Cursor said an attacker obtained an access key Cursor had issued to Fireworks, which Cursor says Fireworks exposed. The key was used to copy a storage bucket of mostly 2025 coding-agent traces from sessions not in Privacy Mode. Some traces held unredacted credentials, affecting fewer than 0.01% of customers. With those, the attacker reached some of Cursor's SaaS tools, archived non-production code, support data and, on October 29, the cache behind Cursor's extension marketplace. There they planted tampered versions 2.1.312 and 2.1.313 of Anthropic's Claude Code extension for about 11 hours. Cursor says Anthropic's product was not at fault, the upstream Open VSX source was clean, and release signing keys were not exposed. It has moved all inference off Fireworks. Security outlets report the payload tried to install a persistent SSH backdoor. Affected users are told to run Cursor's remediation scripts, since uninstalling alone may not be enough. Separately, Notion AI chat transcripts were reported exposed in the same Fireworks breach. Note: Cursor makes the platform this brief runs on, and this account is the company's own. Analysis: the chain, from a vendor's key to training data to credentials to the software supply chain, shows how sharing data with AI vendors creates new paths for attackers.

ASEAN signs its first binding digital-economy pact, covering e-invoices, linked payments and cross-border data

In force May 2027ADB: up to $5B by 2030$368B regional digital GMV, 2026

Ministers in Manila signed the Digital Economy Framework Agreement (DEFA). It is ASEAN's first stand-alone, legally binding digital-economy treaty and replaces the bloc's 2019 e-commerce agreement 180 days after signing. It commits members to accept electronic invoices, shipping documents and signatures, to make national payment systems work together, and to limit rules forcing companies to store commercial and financial data locally. Singapore's trade ministry says exceptions remain for privacy, confidentiality and national security, and countries can implement at different speeds. The Asian Development Bank launched an initiative to raise up to $5 billion by 2030 for payments infrastructure, digital ID and cybersecurity linked to the pact. Google, Temasek and Bain estimate the region's main digital sectors will reach $368 billion in transactions this year. Analysis: the main gain for merchants and logistics firms is less paperwork at borders. Its effect depends on how quickly the ten members actually implement it, which the Singapore Business Federation says is the real test.

TN (opinion, UCEMA) · TN · La Nación · Nov 15

Analysts flag thin Treasury dollar deposits and a heavy peso rollover calendar ahead of 2027

Riesgo país peak 939 (early Nov)Treasury USD deposits < US$200M~$10T pesos to roll this week

The numbers. In a TN column, UCEMA professor Federico Vacalebre notes that country risk hit 939 basis points in early November, its highest in more than a year. It rose 152 points in a week while the Latin American average rose 5. He attributes the jump to the US midterms, in which the opposition won both chambers, and to Brazil's late-October election, on top of stagnant activity since mid-2025. He writes that the Treasury's dollar deposits fell below US$200 million after an interest payment to the IMF, against a year-end target of US$3.7 billion. The Treasury has not managed to place its dollar bond since August. On the peso side, it must roll over just over $10 trillion this week, about $30 trillion at month-end and more than $36 trillion in December. Rollover rates have stayed near 100%, but maturities are getting shorter. He infers from listed amounts that the Treasury bought back and canceled about $1.8 trillion in long inflation-linked bonds; there was no official statement.

Views. Vacalebre argues that the US Treasury's $20 billion swap line is not at immediate risk. Congressional researchers say its purpose is to counter China's influence, which has support from both parties. But he says the swap alone cannot answer how Argentina will cover its dollar needs in an election year. The government's position, repeated by officials, is that fiscal and monetary order must come first and that the debt calendar is manageable. La Nación describes this as the Milei government's most complicated year-end, as it tries to restart its legislative agenda. Country risk eased to about 830 by Friday, per Infobae figures cited in Sunday's brief.
