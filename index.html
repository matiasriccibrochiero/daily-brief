The midterms arrive: a record-low approval rating, a Democratic defection in Michigan, and an Iran decision waiting on the result

Reuters/Ipsos via Investing.com · NPR · BBC · Anadolu · Axios via Anadolu · Nov 1–2
435 House seats35 Senate seats~50M early votesVote Tue, Nov 3

All 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats are on the ballot on Tuesday. The backdrop is difficult for Republicans: a recent Reuters/Ipsos poll put Trump's approval at a record-low 31%, and Reuters/Investing.com cite voter frustration over high gasoline prices and the eight-month war with Iran. Final polling compiled by the New York Times and the BBC suggests Democrats could win well over the 218 seats needed for the House; in the Senate, they are tied or narrowly ahead in the six main battlegrounds, which leaves control too close to call. Nearly 50 million people voted early, a midterm record (Reuters, reported Saturday).

The final weekend brought an unusual break in party lines. Democratic Rep. Haley Stevens, who lost the Michigan Senate primary, formally endorsed Republican nominee Mike Rogers over Democrat Abdul El-Sayed (NPR, Anadolu). Trump held rallies in Iowa and Kansas. Democratic Sen. Chris Murphy of Connecticut was hospitalised after a stroke, his office said; he is not on this year's ballot. The Justice Department also escalated its legal fight with California over a list of alleged non-citizen voters.

Why markets and foreign capitals are watching. Trump has said he will not launch new strikes on Iran before the vote. Axios reported on Sunday that in a Saturday call Israeli PM Benjamin Netanyahu pressed for military action shortly after the election and that Trump preferred to let diplomacy run; an Israeli source called the call "tough," a US source "direct," and Trump described it as "a very good talk." The Wall Street Journal and New York Times have reported US preparations for possible renewed strikes after Nov. 3 (via CNBC).

Why it matters (analysis)If Democrats take one or both chambers, Trump's legislative agenda for his final two years would largely stall, and investors cited by Reuters expect more reliance on executive orders, which keeps policy uncertainty high. A Republican hold would be read as a mandate despite low approval. Pollsters have missed in recent cycles, as the BBC notes, so the margin matters as much as the topline. The result also feeds directly into the Iran decision, which is now the biggest swing factor for oil.

Crypto · Markets

Binance's first day back: $4.65B out, $1.01B in, and no run so far

CNBC · Binance · Bitcoin.com News · AInvest · Nov 1–2
$3.64B net outflow15 networks reopened$1.1B USDC frozenFull proof due Nov 3

Binance said it processed $4.65 billion of withdrawals and $1.01 billion of deposits between 06:00 and 15:30 UTC on Sunday (03:00–12:30 ART), a net outflow of $3.64 billion, or 2.1% of its $171 billion in assets based on Oct. 28 reserve data (CNBC). It says withdrawals spiked at first and then stabilised, and all 15 networks in phase one were open by 12:36 UTC. Founder Changpeng Zhao wrote that "98% choose to stay." Circle said it has frozen more than $1.1 billion in USDC linked to the theft, up from $800 million on Friday.

Independent trackers show a bigger hole. DefiLlama data cited by Bitcoin.com News shows Binance's tracked assets falling from about $144.9 billion on Oct. 29 to $128.3 billion early on Nov. 2, a drop of about 12% that also reflects the stolen funds and lower crypto prices; a screenshot circulating online showed $5.95 billion of net outflows over 24 hours, which Binance has not confirmed. Critics say only a small share of the exchange's 252 listed assets can be withdrawn yet; Binance has given no date for phase two.

The next test is Tuesday. Binance pushed its full zero-knowledge proof of reserves from Monday to "by Tuesday, 2026-11-03 (UTC)," saying it takes 40 to 50 hours to compute. The only public snapshot is from Oct. 31, before withdrawals resumed. AInvest notes that a proof of reserves is narrower than an audit: it shows customer balances were covered at one moment, not the exchange's overall finances.

Why it matters (analysis)Customers took out about a fifth of what they had been locked out of for three days, which is a large number in dollars but small relative to the book; past exchange collapses started with runs that kept accelerating. If Tuesday's proof shows full backing after the outflows, the episode would strengthen the case that the largest exchanges can absorb a multi-billion-dollar theft. A gap, or a long delay on the remaining assets, would revive doubts.

The Decoder via ToolSift · Nov 1

Watermarks become the default: Anthropic and xAI will invisibly mark API text this month

Anthropic emailed API customers that it will watermark output from four older models (Claude Mythos 5, Opus 4.7, Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6), adding to newer models that already carry the mark, reportedly from Nov. 3, with Amazon Bedrock a few days later. On Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6 the mark only applies at default sampling settings, so changing temperature, top_p or top_k produces unmarked text. Its detection tool is in private preview for regulators, researchers and media. xAI says it will watermark Grok API text by default from Nov. 7.

Watermarking helps platforms and regulators trace AI-generated text, which upcoming EU transparency rules require; critics note marks can be removed or avoided, as the sampling loophole shows, and that detection access remains limited.

Munich-based Marble launched with a pre-seed led by Heartcore and Seedcamp. Infobae · Confidencial · Noticias Argentinas · Nov 2

Treasury pays the IMF with almost nothing to spare, as the Board review slips to December

The government owes the IMF about US$800 million on Monday (earlier reports put it at $860–900 million). The Treasury's dollar deposits at the central bank were US$851 million as of Oct. 28, just enough, partly because sales of the Bonar 2029 (AO29) were suspended as country risk climbed (Infobae). It could also buy dollars from the BCRA with its ARS 8.9 trillion in peso deposits, which would cut net reserves. As in recent payments, it is expected to buy the IMF's Special Drawing Rights from the US Treasury; Confidencial reports the payment was made that way, which the Economy Ministry had not confirmed. IMF staff approved the third programme review last week, but the Executive Board will not consider it, and release about $900 million, until early December.

This payment is manageable; the harder one is about $4.5 billion due to bondholders in January, with Treasury deposits near zero and markets effectively closed at nearly 800 bp. That puts the focus on further BCRA purchases and possible multilateral-guaranteed loans.

Iran-Qatar talks run a third day; the two-day Hormuz plan still waits on Washington

Iranian negotiators spent a third straight day with Qatari mediators in Doha on Sunday. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said talks on the "two-day plan," in which the US would take measures on day one and Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz on day two, are "ongoing and progressing" but have reached no conclusion, which "depends on the answers the other side gives." No direct talks with US officials were planned. Washington has not publicly responded; the US military maintains the strait is open. Iran's Revolutionary Guards and defence minister repeated that any attack would meet a response "without geographical limits."

Markets are pricing a higher chance of a deal, but the sequencing gap (Iran wants sanctions relief and frozen funds first) is unchanged, and both sides are preparing militarily for the period after Tuesday.
